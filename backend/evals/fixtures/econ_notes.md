# Supply and demand

The demand curve slopes downward: as price falls, buyers want more of a
good, and as price rises they want less. The supply curve slopes upward:
as price rises, producers are willing to sell more. Market equilibrium
price is the price where the quantity buyers demand exactly equals the
quantity sellers supply, so the two curves cross and there is no leftover
surplus or shortage. For example, if apples sell for one dollar each and
growers offer exactly as many apples as shoppers want to buy at that
price, the apple market has found its equilibrium price. If a bumper
harvest pushes supply outward, price falls until a new equilibrium price
is reached.

# Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand measures how much the quantity demanded of a
good changes when its price changes, expressed as a percentage change in
quantity divided by the percentage change in price. Demand is elastic
when a small price increase causes a large drop in quantity purchased,
and inelastic when quantity barely responds to price at all. Luxury
goods such as vacation travel tend to have elastic demand, since
travelers can easily postpone a trip when airfares climb. Insulin, by
contrast, has inelastic demand, because patients keep buying it at
nearly any price. A seller deciding whether to raise price should first
ask how elastic demand is, since price elasticity of demand determines
whether a price increase adds or subtracts from total revenue.

# Price controls

A binding price ceiling is a legal maximum price set below the
equilibrium price, so it creates a shortage because the quantity
buyers demand at that price exceeds the quantity sellers are willing to
supply. Rent control in many cities is a binding price ceiling: tenants
pay less, but landlords supply fewer units, so some renters cannot find
housing at all. A price floor set above equilibrium price instead
creates a surplus, as with agricultural price supports where the
government buys the unsold surplus. A price ceiling set above the
equilibrium price, or a price floor set below it, has no effect, since
the market can already reach that price on its own.

# Marginal analysis

A profit-maximizing firm keeps producing extra units as long as each
one adds more to revenue than it adds to cost, and stops exactly where
marginal revenue equals marginal cost. Marginal cost is the added cost
of producing one more unit of output, while marginal revenue is the
added revenue from selling that unit. A bakery deciding whether to bake
one more loaf of bread should compare the cost of the flour and oven
time against the price the loaf will fetch; once marginal cost climbs
above marginal revenue, baking another loaf destroys profit rather than
adding to it. This rule, marginal revenue equals marginal cost, is the
single most useful test of whether a firm's output level is efficient.

# Market structures

A monopoly is a market with a single seller and no close substitutes,
letting the firm set price above marginal cost and earn sustained
economic profit. Perfect competition sits at the other extreme: many
small firms sell an identical product, none can influence price alone,
and price is driven down to marginal cost in the long run. Monopolistic
competition falls between the two, with many firms selling similar but
differentiated products, like local restaurants, each holding a sliver
of pricing power from its own brand. Oligopoly describes a market
structure dominated by a few large firms, such as commercial aircraft
manufacturers, where each firm's pricing decisions visibly affect its
rivals.

# Externalities

An externality is a cost or benefit of a transaction that falls on a
third party who did not choose to be part of it. Pollution from a
factory is a classic negative externality: the factory and its
customers do not pay for the smoke that nearby residents breathe,
which drives production above the socially optimal level. Vaccination
is a positive externality, since a vaccinated person also protects
others from catching the disease, so unregulated markets under-provide
vaccines relative to the optimal level. Governments respond to a
negative externality with taxes or regulation and to a positive
externality with subsidies, aiming to align private incentives with the
deadweight loss that externalities otherwise create for society.
